How the calculation works

Material requirements planning is four arithmetic steps applied in a fixed order. The order matters — doing MOQ rounding before netting, for example, produces systematically inflated purchase orders.

Step 1 — BOM explosion (gross requirement)

Gross requirement = FG order qty × qty per unit × (1 + scrap %)

Scrap belongs here rather than as a later adjustment, because losses compound down the structure. If you lose 2% of caps at the capping line, you needed 2% more caps to start with, not 2% more finished units.

Step 2 — Netting

Net requirement = max( 0, gross requirement − on hand − on order )

Open purchase orders are the line most often left out, and leaving it out is how the same material gets bought twice. If a purchase order is already in transit for that component, it is supply — as real as stock in the rack, just later.

Step 3 — Lot sizing (MOQ)

Order quantity = ceiling( net requirement ÷ MOQ ) × MOQ

This is where excess inventory is created, and it is unavoidable rather than a mistake. What matters is knowing it is happening: a net requirement of 300 against an MOQ of 5,000 means you are buying sixteen times what this order needs, and that surplus is now a carrying-cost decision, not a purchasing one.

Step 4 — Back-scheduling

Production start = required date − production lead time
Order-by date = production start − component lead time
Slack = order-by date − today

Negative slack is the whole point of the exercise. It says the date cannot be met through that component regardless of how much you expedite everything else — and it identifies which component to negotiate, substitute or air-freight rather than expediting the whole order.

Where this model breaks

  • It assumes deterministic lead times. Real lead times are distributions, and right-skewed ones. Use a high percentile rather than the average for anything on the critical path, or you will be late roughly half the time by construction.
  • It is single-level. A real BOM has sub-assemblies with their own lead times, and the explosion cascades. For a two-level structure, run the tool once per level and chain the dates.
  • It ignores capacity. Material availability is necessary but not sufficient — you can be fully material-ready and still line-constrained. Check the production calendar alongside this, not after it.
  • It nets against total stock, not available-to-promise. If some of that on-hand stock is already committed to another order, your true availability is lower than this shows.
  • Shelf life is not modelled. Buying sixteen times your requirement because of MOQ is a different decision for a component with a two-year shelf life than for one with six months.

Frequently asked

Why is my order-by date in the past?

Because the required date is not achievable through that component. The tool is telling you the truth rather than smoothing it. The options are: move the required date, find a shorter-lead-time source, substitute the component, or split the order so the constrained portion ships later.

Should MOQ excess count as a planning failure?

No — it is a structural feature of supplier economics. It becomes a failure only when nobody accounts for it, and the surplus quietly becomes aged stock nobody has budgeted to carry.
